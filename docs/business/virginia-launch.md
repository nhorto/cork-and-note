# Virginia launch — go-to-market

**Decided:** 2026-09-08 · **Owner:** Nick · Companion to [`launch-plan-2026-09.md`](launch-plan-2026-09.md) §5

Virginia is the launch region. This document is the operational half: who to approach, what to hand them, and what to say. The strategic case lives in the launch plan; everything here is meant to be used as-is rather than rewritten.

> **A note on the shortlist.** §3 names real wineries, but a name is only a starting point. Tasting-room managers change, hours change, and a winery that took a card in March may have a policy against them in September. §2 gives the selection criteria and §3.4 gives a repeatable way to rebuild the list, so it stays useful after the specific names go stale.

---

## 1. What the channel actually is

A QR card on a tasting-room bar reaches someone **at the exact moment of intent**: they have just tasted five wines, they liked the third one, and in ninety minutes they will not remember which it was. That is the entire product in one sentence, and it is a moment no app-store listing, ad or influencer can buy.

It is also the one channel a bigger competitor cannot copy at speed. Vivino cannot walk into forty Virginia tasting rooms; a person who lives two hours away can.

Three things follow from that, and they shape everything below:

1. **The card sells the moment, not the app.** "Remember what you tasted today" beats any feature list.
2. **The winery has to get something.** A tasting room does not put a stranger's marketing on the bar as a favour. §4.2 is built around what they get.
3. **Conversion is measured in installs per card per week**, not in impressions. §5 covers instrumenting it, which today is a genuine gap.

---

## 2. How to choose which wineries to approach

Rank candidates on these, in order. The first two matter far more than the rest.

1. **Tasting-room traffic, especially weekend traffic.** A card on a bar that serves 400 people on a Saturday is worth ten cards on bars that serve 40. Proxies you can check without asking: does it take reservations for weekends, does it have an events calendar, does it have a large indoor space or a pavilion, does it show up in "best of" roundups.
2. **A seated or bar-side tasting flight**, rather than counter-pour-and-wander. The card only works if there is a surface in front of a person who is sitting still with several wines to compare.
3. **Drive time from you.** Placement is not a one-off — you will want to go back, and the wineries that say yes are also the ones worth filming at. Somewhere you will genuinely return to beats somewhere impressive that you will not.
4. **A tasting room that is a destination in itself** — a view, a restaurant, live music, weddings. Destination visitors are on a day out, take photos, and are more likely to install something.
5. **Cluster density.** A cluster lets you place five cards in one afternoon and makes a "we're also at X and Y down the road" line true, which is the single most persuasive thing you can say to the sixth.
6. **Owner-operated over corporate.** A small winery's decision-maker is often behind the bar; a larger one routes you to a marketing coordinator and a two-week wait.

**Two anti-signals.** Skip anywhere that runs a formal brand-partnership program (you will be quoted a rate card), and skip anywhere the tasting room is clearly not the business — production-first wineries selling mostly wholesale have no bar traffic to reach.

### 3.2 The wedge — what Virginia wineries say their problem is

The strongest argument for the QR card is not a claim about Cork & Note. It is the wineries' own words, from the **[2024 Wine Industry Report, GO Virginia Region 9](https://www.govirginia9.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/2024-Virginia-Wine-Industry-Report.pdf)** (December 2024, surveying calendar 2023; 59 responses from the Charlottesville/Piedmont counties, described as covering nearly a third of the state's vineyards and wineries):

- **75% named tasting-room visitor traffic as a concern.** Many report that they "not only lost their pandemic era gains, but also continue to struggle to return to their pre-pandemic visitation numbers."
- **72% of all sales go through the tasting room.**
- **43% named marketing as a concern**, specifically citing a need for better online marketing, social and SEO.

So three quarters of them are worried about the room the card sits in, and nearly half are worried about exactly the kind of reach a free card offers. **That is the sentence to open a conversation with** — not a feature.

### 3.5 How to rebuild this list when it goes stale

Names age; the method does not.

1. **Start from the association rosters, not from search.** [Loudoun Wineries & Vineyards Association](https://www.loudounwine.org/wineries) publishes all 51 members. The [Monticello Wine Trail](https://monticellowinetrail.com/), [Shenandoah Valley Wine Trail](https://www.shenandoahvalleywinetrail.com/members/) and [Chesapeake Bay Wine Trail](https://chesapeakebaywinetrail.com/) publish theirs. These are maintained by people with a reason to keep them current.
2. **Filter by the §2 criteria**, in order. Traffic and drive time first.
3. **Check three things per candidate, all from their own site:** current tasting-room hours (not the trail directory's), whether tastings are seated or walk-up, and whether there is a named events or marketing contact.
4. **Award lists are a free traffic proxy.** The Virginia Governor's Cup case (announced each March; the 2026 round drew a record 670+ entries and gave 224 golds to 113 producers) and the county-level awards reliably surface wineries that are investing in themselves — which correlates with investing in the tasting room.
5. **Verify AVA assignment against the winery's own address**, never against a third-party list. Published AVA rosters contain real errors — one widely-mirrored source files a Fauquier County winery under the Eastern Shore AVA, 200 miles away.

## 4. What to hand them

### 4.1 The QR card

Physical spec: **a 3×3 in or 3.5×2 in card**, matte, heavy stock, standing in a small acrylic holder or laid flat by the tasting sheet. Not a flyer, not a rack card — it has to survive a wet bar and look like it belongs next to a tasting menu. Print a **short URL under the QR code** as well: some people will not scan a code from a stranger, and a few phones will not focus at bar-top distance.

**Front — primary version.** Aimed at the taster, in their moment.

> ## Which one was your favourite?
>
> You will not remember by dinner.
>
> **Cork & Note** — scan the label, rate it, keep the note.
> Free to start. Works offline in the tasting room.
>
> `[QR]`
> corkandnote.com

**Front — alternative, if the winery prefers something warmer.** Some tasting rooms will find the line above too blunt; this one performs the same job without the implied criticism.

> ## Take the tasting room home with you
>
> **Cork & Note** keeps a note of every wine you taste and every place you taste it — so next time you know exactly which one you loved.
>
> `[QR]`
> Free to start · corkandnote.com

**Front — the shortest version**, for a card that has to sit in a crowded space.

> ### Remember what you tasted today.
> `[QR]` **Cork & Note** · free wine journal

**Back — for the winery, and for the sceptical taster.**

> **What it does**
> Scan a label or your tasting sheet, rate the wine, add a note. Your visit to *[Winery Name]* is saved with it — so a year from now you know what you liked here, and can find it again.
>
> No ads. No score-chasing. Your notes stay yours, and you can delete everything at any time.
>
> Built by one person in Maryland who kept forgetting which wine he liked.

**Copy rules if you rewrite any of this.**

- **Never imply drinking more.** The pitch is memory, not consumption. This matters both for tasting-room goodwill and for the App Store's 18+ framing.
- **Never rate or rank the winery's wines on the card.** You are a guest on their bar.
- **Do not print a price.** "Free to start" is accurate and does not date; a price on a card outlives the price.
- **Name the winery on the back if they will let you.** A card that says *[Winery Name]* is a card about them, which is why it stays on the bar.

### 4.2 What the winery gets — say this explicitly

They are giving up bar space. Be concrete about the return, and do not overpromise things the app does not do yet.

- **Their visitors remember which wine they liked** — which is the single biggest driver of a later online order or a repeat visit. Anyone in a tasting room has watched someone squint at a tasting sheet in the parking lot.
- **Their winery page in the app carries their name, their pin on the map, and the visitor's own notes about the visit.** Every future tasting the visitor logs there strengthens it.
- **A photo and a mention** when you film at their tasting room, if they want one.
- **Zero cost, zero commitment, and you take the cards away if they ask.** Say that sentence out loud; it removes the whole objection.

**Do not promise** analytics, visitor counts, sponsored placement or referral revenue. None of that exists — it is the B2B line in the June strategy doc, and it needs consumer scale first. Promising it now is how a good relationship gets burned in month three.

### 4.3 The outreach email

Short, specific, no attachments, and a clear ask that costs them nothing. Send it to the tasting-room manager or events contact, not to a generic info@ address if you can avoid it. **Subject lines that work:** "A small card for your tasting bar" or "Quick question from a Maryland wine app".

> **Subject:** A small card for your tasting bar
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> I'm Nick — I live in Maryland and I've been visiting Virginia wineries for years. I kept getting home from a tasting and not being able to remember which wine I actually liked, so I built an app to fix it: **Cork & Note**, a wine journal that lets you scan a label or a tasting sheet, rate the wine, and save it against the winery you were at.
>
> It's launching this autumn and I'm starting in Virginia. I'd like to leave a small card by your tasting bar — about the size of a business card, with a QR code on it — so your guests can keep a note of what they tasted at [Winery]. That's the whole ask. It's free, there's nothing for you to sign, and I'll take them away the moment they're in your way.
>
> What's in it for you: people remember which of your wines they liked, which is usually what turns a tasting into an online order later. Your winery gets its own page in the app with your guests' own notes on it.
>
> If it's easier to talk about in person, I'm out that way most weekends and would happily come and taste properly — I'd rather buy a flight and hand you the cards myself than keep emailing.
>
> Thanks for reading,
> Nick Horton
> Cork & Note · [support address] · corkandnote.com

**Follow up exactly once**, after 7–10 days, with two sentences: "Just checking this didn't get buried — happy to drop cards off in person any weekend." Then stop. A tasting room that ignores two emails is a no, and the region is not short of wineries.

### 4.4 The in-person script

Better than email, and the reason to build the shortlist by drive time. **Buy the tasting first.** You are a customer who has a thing, not a salesperson with a pitch.

> *(after the flight, to whoever poured — and ask this while paying, not while they are pouring for someone else)*
>
> "Can I ask you something odd? I built a wine journal app — it's for exactly this, remembering which one you liked once you get home. I'm putting little cards with a QR code on them in tasting rooms around Virginia. Would you mind if I left one on the bar? It's free, there's nothing to sign, and if it's in the way just bin it."

Then **stop talking**. If they say yes, hand over two cards, not twenty. If they hesitate, ask who to email and leave it there. If they say the owner decides, ask for a name and use §4.3.

**If they ask what's in it for them**, use §4.2's first bullet and nothing else — one clear benefit lands harder than four.

**Bring:** a small stack of cards, an acrylic holder or two (offering the holder makes yes easier — it means they do not have to find one), and the app open on your phone in case they want to see it. Do not bring a laptop.

### 4.5 The wine-trail and association route

Approaching a trail or a county tourism body is one conversation instead of fifteen, and an introduction from one converts far better than a cold email. It is also slower and more likely to end in "put it in the newsletter" than in cards on bars.

**Do both, in this order:** get five wineries to say yes first, then approach the trail with "five of your members already have these" — which is a completely different conversation from "may I market to your members".

Ranked by leverage:

| Organisation | Why | Reach |
|---|---|---|
| **[Loudoun Wineries & Vineyards Association](https://www.loudounwine.org/wineries)** | The highest-leverage single contact in the state, and it covers the cluster nearest to you | 51 member wineries in one county |
| **[Chesapeake Bay Wine Trail](https://chesapeakebaywinetrail.com/)** | **Small enough to cover completely**, publishes all seven members with phone numbers, and **already runs a free mobile tasting-pass program** — so the mechanic needs no explaining | 7 wineries, Northern Neck |
| **[Virginia Wineries Association](https://www.vawine.org/)** | The statewide trade body, with a formal affiliate tier for non-winery businesses. The legitimate front door to everyone | Statewide |
| **[Monticello Wine Trail](https://monticellowinetrail.com/)** | 40+ wineries around Charlottesville. ⚠️ No public contact page — route in through the VWA or through a member you already know | 40+ wineries |
| **[Shenandoah Valley Wine Trail](https://www.shenandoahvalleywinetrail.com/members/)** | Makes a thinly-spread region worth one trip | ~23 wineries |
| **[Visit Loudoun](https://www.visitloudoun.org/food-and-drink/wine-country)** | The destination marketing organisation rather than the trade body — a second, different door into the same 50+ rooms | 50+ rooms |
| **Virginia Wine Board Marketing Office** | State-funded, huge reach, prints 175,000 winery guides a year — **but see §4A.2 before pitching** | Statewide |

**One date worth planning around: October is Virginia Wine Month**, the state marketing office's flagship promotion, run statewide every year. It is the single best moment to launch into. October 2026 is about three weeks out from this decision, which is too tight for a print run plus winery sign-ups — so treat this October as a **pilot** with a handful of hand-delivered cards, and aim the real push at October 2027.

Multi-winery festivals (the Fall Town Point Virginia Wine Festival in Norfolk, mid-October, gathers 25+ Virginia wineries in one park) are excellent for **meeting winery owners in one afternoon** and terrible for consumer acquisition — festival pours are crowded, small and story-free, which is the opposite of the moment the card is designed for. **Go to festivals to sell wineries, not to sell tasters.**

## 4A. Two things to know before the first card is printed

### 4A.1 Virginia ABC — the rule that mattered was repealed

**No Virginia ABC rule was found that stops a non-licensee leaving a non-alcohol promotional card in a farm winery tasting room.** The regulation that used to constrain interior advertising most directly, [3VAC5-20-20](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/admincode/title3/agency5/chapter20/section20/), now reads simply *"(Repealed.)"* — repealed effective **29 January 2025** as part of a broad overhaul that also repealed §§ 50, 60, 70 and 80 of the same chapter. What survives ([3VAC5-20-10](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/admincode/title3/agency5/chapter20/section10/)) governs *alcoholic beverage advertising by licensees and permittees*, which Cork & Note is not. The three-tier and tied-house rules exist to police manufacturers, wholesalers and retailers of alcohol — none of which describe this app either.

**⚠️ One page could not be read.** Virginia ABC's own plain-language advertising guidance at `abc.virginia.gov/licenses/retail-resources/advertising` returned HTTP 403 to automated access. **Read it manually before a large print run**, and consider one call to an ABC special agent, which is what ABC itself recommends for fact-specific questions.

Four practical rules that follow, and which the §4.1 copy already obeys:

- **No alcohol imagery, no bottle graphics, nothing that reads as promoting consumption.** "Remember what you tasted today" is comfortably clear of the line; a picture of a full glass starts an argument you do not need.
- **Never name a wine, a vintage, or a price on the card.** That is the step that turns a consumer utility into alcohol advertising.
- **Give the winery nothing of value in exchange for placement** — no payment, no revenue share, no free product. §4.2 is deliberately built on non-monetary benefits, and this is why.
- **There is precedent.** The Chesapeake Bay Wine Trail already runs a **free mobile tasting-pass program** across its member wineries, so a third-party digital pass in a Virginia tasting room is demonstrably a thing that already happens.

### 4A.2 The state marketing office is building something adjacent

Worth knowing before walking into that particular meeting: the Virginia Wine Board's [FY 2024–25 annual report](https://www.vdacs.virginia.gov/pdf/winebdannualreport.pdf) says VirginiaWine.org drew over 200,000 visitors in FY25 (up 19%) and gained **"new features like tracking winery visits."**

That is not the same product — a state directory tracking visits is not a personal tasting journal with a sommelier — but it is close enough that the Virginia Wine Board Marketing Office is **either a distribution partner or a competitor, and not a neutral channel.** Decide which before pitching them. The same office prints **175,000 copies of the Virginia Winery Guide** a year, which is proof that physical distribution into tasting rooms works at scale and is exactly the kind of reach worth partnering for.

The **[Virginia Wineries Association](https://www.vawine.org/)** is the cleaner front door: it is the statewide trade body, it runs the Governor's Cup, and it has a formal affiliate/associate membership tier for non-winery businesses. No software vendors are currently listed in it — which is either an opening or a signal, and worth one email to find out.

## 5. Measuring it — a real gap

**Right now there is no way to tell a card apart from an ad.** The app has no attribution: no install referrer, no campaign parameter, no per-winery code. If forty cards go out and installs rise, nothing in the product can say which wineries did it, and the second print run will be a guess.

Options, cheapest first, none of them built:

1. **A distinct short link per winery** (or per cluster) on the card, redirecting to the App Store. The redirect's own logs answer "how many scans", which is most of the value, and it needs nothing in the app.
2. **Apple's App Store campaign links** — free, and they report installs per campaign in App Store Connect. One campaign per winery cluster.
3. **A "where did you hear about us" question after the first tasting is logged.** Cheap, self-reported, and the only one that survives a phone that blocks referrers.

Option 1 is worth having **before the first cards are printed**, because it is the only one that cannot be added retroactively to a card already on a bar. Flag it to the engineering lane — it is not a docs change.

## 6. Sequence

| When | Do |
|---|---|
| Before printing | Pick the short-link scheme (§5.1) · confirm the domain · get the support address onto the card |
| First trip | Two clusters, one day. Buy tastings. Aim for 5 yeses, not 15 attempts |
| Week after | Email the ones you could not reach in person (§4.3) · follow up once, 7–10 days later |
| Once 5 are placed | Approach the trail / tourism body with those five as proof (§4.5) |
| Every visit after | Film one vertical video (launch plan §5) · check the scan counts · re-stock cards |

**One measurable goal for the first month: five wineries with cards on the bar, and a scan number you trust.** Forty placements with no attribution is a worse outcome than five with it.
